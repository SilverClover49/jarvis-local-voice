01 · the brain02 / 10

Local AI means the model runs
on your machine.

Not a website you talk to. A file on your disk, loaded into memory, doing its arithmetic on your own CPU while you wait for it.

No networkTurn the wifi off. It still answers.
No accountNo key, no quota, no per-message bill.
No GPUIt runs on the CPU. Slower than a graphics card, and it runs.
Qwen 3.5
qwen3.5-0.8b-q4_k_m.gguf
533 MBon disk
parameters0.8 B
quantisationQ4_K_M
a reply takes0.36–0.73 s

0.8B parameters is a count of numbers — eight hundred million of them, learned from text. That is the entire file. No database, no copy of the internet, nothing to look anything up in.

Q4_K_M is how tightly those numbers are packed: roughly four bits each instead of sixteen. It is the only reason 800 million of them fit in 533 MB and still run on a laptop CPU.

Cloud models are the same arithmetic, larger, on someone else's CPU. The arithmetic is not the difference. The size is.

02 · inside the brain03 / 10

A transformer is attention.
Attention is multiply and add.

"the cat sat on the mat because it was tired" row → column

Illustrative — the shape of the pattern, not weights logged from the model. Hover a row to see what one word looks at.

Every one of those decisions is a number, and the model holds them as a grid: word against word. The grid is the model's only way of connecting two words. The top half is blacked out — at word four it is not allowed to see word five. It predicts forward, one word at a time.

A
× B
= C
a₁×b₁+ a₂×b₂+ a₃×b₃

One number in the output = one row × one column: multiply each pair, add them up. That is the whole operation, and it is all a CPU does anyway — the transformer just does a great many of them.

03 · context04 / 10

Context is the text in front of it. Nothing else.

The model has no memory between calls. Every turn, the page re-sends the system prompt plus every turn so far. That list is the context — the window the model can see at once.

everything said so far, in orderempty room · it writes here

Three exchanges in, and all six messages get re-sent every turn — before the model has written a word of the next answer.

workload, turn by turn

Relative work, if every turn adds the same amount of text. Squares, not measured milliseconds.

Every word in the window is compared with every other word. Twice the text is about four times the work, so the sixth turn is not six times slower than the first — it is closer to thirty-six times the work. That is what "remember six turns" costs.

And the reply cannot start until the whole window has been read. The pause you feel before it speaks is the window, not the typing. Longer memory, longer pause — every single turn.

04 · the ears · 1 of 205 / 10

It hears with Whisper. 32 MB.

Whisper tiny.en
ggml-tiny.en-q5_1.bin · 32 MB
What it isOpenAI's speech recogniser, compiled to run locally by whisper.cpp.
SpeedAbout 0.5 s for a sentence of a few seconds, on this CPU.
LanguageEnglish only. That is what the .en means.
why not the browser's own speech API

webkitSpeechRecognition is four lines of code and it works.

It also streams your microphone to Google's servers. In a session about models that live on your own disk, that would be a lie I would have to talk around. So the ear is a file too — nothing you say leaves the laptop, ever, including when you are offline.

The honest cost: tiny.en is the smallest Whisper. It mishears names, numbers and Indian English far more often than the large ones. You are trading accuracy for 32 MB, and you should know which side of that trade you are on.

04 · the ears · 2 of 206 / 10

Sound becomes one long list of numbers.

microphone · 44,100 samples a second one sentence · about 3 s
01 · RESAMPLE

We down-sample to 16 kHz, the rate Whisper was trained on. One second of speech becomes 16,000 numbers instead of 44,100. 16 kHz already covers every frequency a voice actually uses.

02 · PACK

The browser records WebM, and whisper.cpp refuses WebM. So the page writes a WAV header by hand and hands over raw 16-bit samples. Thirty lines of arithmetic in app.js.

03 · RECOGNISE

Those numbers go to the ear as one HTTP request. What comes back is a string — plain text, no punctuation, lower case — which the brain never knows was ever audio.

the ear returns
whatisseventeentimes twentythree
→ "what is seventeen times twenty three"

Kokoro sounds better than Piper. I measured both on this laptop. Kokoro generates slower than the audio plays — a ten-second answer needs eleven seconds to make, before a single word comes out of the speaker. That is not a conversation; that is loading.

Piper is not the best voice available. It is the best voice that keeps up. That is the whole decision.

05 · the voice · 2 of 208 / 10

Text becomes phonemes. Phonemes become air.

"Good evening, sir." →
ɡʊd · iːvnɪŋ · sɜː
24 kHz waveform · 16-bit mono 0.3–0.5 s to generate

Piper is two models wearing one coat. First espeak-ng turns English spelling into phonemes — how the word sounds, not how it is written. Then a small VITS model turns phonemes into a waveform, sample by sample.

That split is why it says "live" and "give" differently, and why it can read almost any English word it has never seen. It still has no idea what any of it means: wrong stress, flat emotion, and it will read an acronym as if it were a word. On the other hand it never stumbles, never repeats itself, and never stalls — which is what you actually notice in a demo.

06 · what holds it together09 / 10

Three servers, three ports,
one address.

Nothing here is glued together. Each one is a separate program that listens on a port. The page is just the thing that calls them in order.

All three bind to 127.0.0.1 — "this computer". There is no other address to call, no key to send, and no way for the request to leave the machine.

POST 127.0.0.1:8081/inference wav → text
POST 127.0.0.1:5001/api/v1/generate text → text
POST 127.0.0.1:5002/say text → wav

Three curl commands. That is the entire backend — if the page breaks, the assistant still answers from a terminal.
